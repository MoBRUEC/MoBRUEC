# The Threshold Test

## Five questions that separate AI value from AI activity

**88% of organisations use AI in at least one function. 39% report any EBIT contribution at all — and most of those put it below five percent of EBIT.** Filter for material impact and McKinsey lands on 6% as high performers. BCG, measuring the same gap on its own scale, lands on 5%.

Adoption is easy to count. Value needs a **threshold** — and the threshold has to be set **before** the work starts. That is the whole method. Five questions, answered in writing, dated, before the first pilot is funded.

## The five questions

### 01 — What is the threshold, in writing, before you start?

McKinsey's own line for a high performer is **more than five percent of EBIT attributed to AI**. Pick your number. Write it down. Date it.

**Catches:** success declared against a bar invented after the results arrived.

### 02 — Whose P&L?

**Enterprise level, or the unit where the system runs.** The 39% figure is enterprise level — and the gap between unit-level wins and group-level P&L is where most business cases quietly fail.

**Catches:** a unit-level win that never reaches the group P&L.

### 03 — What does the money replace?

**Headcount not hired. Contracts not renewed. Cycle time taken out of a process someone owns.** If nothing gets smaller, nothing was saved.

**Catches:** productivity gains with no line item that moves.

### 04 — What breaks when it scales?

Every system that reaches production inherits **a data boundary, an audit obligation, and a failure mode with a named owner**. Where no one owns the failure, the system does not leave the pilot — and the value never arrives.

**Catches:** the pilot that cannot pass its own risk review.

### 05 — What is the kill condition?

**Which pilots stop, on what evidence, by what date.** Decided in advance, by someone with the authority to stop them.

**Catches:** a portfolio that only ever grows.

## Scoring

- **Five in writing today** — you are measuring value.
- **Three or four** — you have a business case and no verdict.
- **Two or fewer** — you are counting activity.

---

## Why a threshold, and not a business case

A business case argues forward. A threshold judges backward. The 88/39/6 pattern exists because organisations happily do the first and never build the second — adoption gets announced, value gets asserted, and nobody is ever on record having said what number would count.

Five questions, one page, ten minutes per pilot. The expensive part is not answering them. It is discovering, a year in, that nobody did.
